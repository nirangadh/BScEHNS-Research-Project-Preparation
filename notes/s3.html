Session 3 notes

Methodology, Tooling and Evaluation Design

These are the notes for Session 3. They show you how to choose a method, how to justify your tools, and how to design the test that will show whether your project worked.

In this page you will learn
  • the three method families, and how to name yours
  • the difference between listing your tools and justifying them
  • the three parts of every evaluation design: a metric, a baseline and an environment
  • how to find those three parts inside your own research question
  • what makes a design sketch ready to hand to someone else, and what a testbed is for

1. Why this session matters

In plain English

A methodology chapter says what you will build and how. It must also say how you will know that it worked. That second part is the evaluation, and it is the part most often left out.

The main idea of this session is one sentence: your evaluation design is already written into your research question. If you cannot find it there, the question needs more work, not the evaluation.

This session also opens the way to ethics approval. You apply in Week 7, and you can only apply when your method is roughly fixed.

If the question does not carry a metric, a comparison and an environment, there is nothing for the evaluation to measure, and the empty space fills up with build steps instead. Method is where an unframed question finally becomes undeliverable.

2. Choosing a method

In plain English

Nearly every technical project at this level uses one of three kinds of method. The notes call them method families.

Each family supports a different kind of claim. So the family you name should match what your question claims.

The notes are direct about the weight of this section: name your family correctly, justify it briefly, and spend your effort on the sections that follow.

Words you will meet in this section

WordWhat it means
Method familyA group of research methods that work in the same basic way.
TreatmentThe change that you apply in an experiment. In Chathura's project it is the set of secure settings.
ControlThe version that you leave unchanged, so that you have something to compare with.
OutcomeThe result that you measure after the treatment.
Causal claimA claim that one thing produced another: the treatment caused the change.
ArtefactIn design-science: the thing that you build. It can be a tool, a model or a way of working.
IncumbentThe one that is in place now. The incumbent approach is how the job is done today.
Proof-of-conceptA small working version, built to show that an idea can work.
Comparative performanceHow well one thing does when it is measured beside another.
ArchetypeA basic type that many examples follow. Here: one of the four types of project from Session 1.
ArbiterThe one who decides when there is doubt.
Analytical evaluationTesting a design by reasoning or calculation, without building all of it.
SimulationRunning a model of a system in software, in place of the real system.
FeasiblePossible to do with the time and equipment that you have.

Three method families cover almost all technical projects at this level.

  • Experimental. A treatment is applied and an outcome is measured against a control. Supports a causal claim within the environment tested.
  • Design-science. An artefact is built and evaluated against a defined need. Supports a claim that the artefact does something better than the incumbent approach.
  • Build-and-evaluate, or proof-of-concept. Something is constructed and then measured against a baseline. Supports a claim of comparative performance.
The three families side by side
FamilyWhat you doThe claim it supports
ExperimentalChange one thing, keep the rest the same, and measure before and after.The change caused the result, inside the system that you tested.
Design-scienceBuild something for a stated need, and test it against that need.What you built does the job better than the way it is done now.
Build-and-evaluateBuild something, and measure it beside a baseline.What you built performs better or worse than the baseline, by a stated amount.

Naming your family matters, but it is not where marks are won or lost. Name it accurately, justify it in two or three sentences, and spend your effort on tools, architecture and evaluation.

Phrases explained
where marks are won or lost
The picture: A match that is decided in one part of the field.
Why it works: Every part of the chapter counts, but the parts do not count equally. The notes tell you where the result is decided: in tools, in the design of the system, and in the evaluation. Naming the family is not that place.
Where it stops working: In a match the other side tries to stop you. Nobody does here. A mark is lost only by leaving the work undone.

2.1 Matching method to archetype

The mapping below is a default, not a rule. Your question, not your archetype, is the final arbiter.

Phrases explained
a default, not a rule
The picture: A new phone arrives with settings already chosen. You may change them.
Why it works: The list gives the usual family for each type of project. It is a sensible place to start. If your question needs a different family, choose that one and say why.
Where it stops working: You can keep a phone's default settings without ever thinking about them. Here you cannot. Even when you keep the usual family, you still have to justify it in two or three sentences.
the final arbiter
The picture: Two players disagree, and the referee decides.
Why it works: If the usual family for your project type and the claim in your question point in different directions, the question decides. The family must fit what the question claims.
Where it stops working: A referee is a person and can explain a decision. A question cannot. You have to read your own question and work out what kind of claim it makes.
  • Detection/monitoring tool (E1 Tharindu): build-and-evaluate. A detector is constructed and then measured against a baseline on defined traffic. The claim is comparative performance, so the method must produce numbers.
  • Hardening/defence framework (E2 Chathura): experimental. A treatment (the baseline configuration) is applied and an outcome (technique success rate) is measured before and after against a control. The claim is causal within the testbed.
  • Security-testing methodology (E3 Dilini): design-science with an experimental evaluation. The artefact is the methodology itself; the evaluation demonstrates it is better than the incumbent practice on a defined measure.
  • Protocol/architecture design: design-science, typically with analytical or simulation-based evaluation. Building the full protocol stack is rarely feasible in the time available. The trap to teach here: students choose this archetype and then propose an experimental evaluation they cannot run, which is a feasibility failure that surfaces at Criterion 3 and again at Criterion 4.
Go deeper

The last item in the list carries a warning. A full protocol stack is rarely something one student can build in the time. So the usual evaluation for this type of project is by reasoning or by simulation.

The trap is to choose this type and then promise an experiment on a system that you cannot build. The notes say that this weakness shows twice: under Criterion 3, which looks at method, and again under Criterion 4. So one over-ambitious promise costs you in two places. If your project is of this type, write down what you can really run before you write the evaluation.

Chathura applies secure settings to a test system and measures attack success before and after. Which family is this, and what kind of claim does it support?

Experimental. A treatment (the settings) is applied, and an outcome (how many attack techniques succeed) is measured against a control. It supports a causal claim: the settings caused the change. The notes add the limit: within the testbed.

3. Justifying tools

In plain English

To list a tool is to say that you will use it. To justify a tool is to argue that it is the right one for this project.

The notes give three signs of a real justification, and call them tells. Then they show two versions of one paragraph by Chathura: a weak one that lists, and a strong one that justifies.

One warning about a word. In this section a "baseline" is a published set of secure settings. In Section 4 a "baseline" is what you compare your result with.

Words you will meet in this section

WordWhat it means
JustificationAn argument that a choice is the right one, with reasons.
AlternativesThe other things that you could have chosen.
CriterionA test that you use to judge or to choose. The plural is criteria. In this module, Criterion 3 is also the name of one row of the marking rubric.
ContextThe situation of this one project: its organisation, its equipment, its question.
ReputationWhat people in general say about something. "Everyone uses it" is a reputation.
Configuration baselineA published set of recommended secure settings for a system. This is not the baseline of an evaluation.
CIS BenchmarksSets of secure settings published by CIS, an independent security organisation. There is one for each product.
DISA STIGsSets of secure settings written for the defence systems of the United States.
Microsoft Security BaselinesSets of secure settings that Microsoft publishes for its own products.
Group PolicyThe Windows feature that applies settings to many computers from one place.
NIST CSFThe Cybersecurity Framework of NIST, a standards body of the United States. Protect is one of its functions.
Control frameworkAn organised list of security measures (controls) that an organisation should have.
PrescriptiveSaying exactly what must be done, with little freedom.
SMEA small or medium-sized enterprise: a business that is not large.
EstateAll the computers and systems that one organisation runs.
TraceableCan be followed, step by step, from one end to the other.
DivergeTo go in different directions. Two baselines diverge where they recommend different settings.
PaddedMade longer with words that add nothing.

A justification is not a description of what a tool is. It is an argument for why this tool, in this project, in this environment, in preference to the alternatives you considered. There are three tells of a real one:

Phrases explained
three tells
The picture: A card player always touches one ear when the cards are good. The other players learn to watch for it.
Why it works: In card games a tell is a small sign that shows what a player really holds. The notes use the word for a sign that shows what a paragraph really is. If the three signs are present, the paragraph is a justification. If they are absent, it is a list, however confident it sounds.
Where it stops working: A card player's tell is an accident, and the player would hide it if possible. These three are the opposite: you put them into your paragraph on purpose. And a player can fake a tell. You cannot fake the third one for long, because a criterion taken from your own project can be checked against your project.
  • Alternatives are named.
  • A criterion for choosing between them is stated.
  • That criterion is derived from this project's own context, not from the tool's general reputation.

3.1 The worked pair, on Chathura at Pahana Retail

Chathura's project is a hardening project for a retail SME. [Illustrative example - do not cite]

Listing (weak)

The project will use CIS Benchmarks, DISA STIGs and Microsoft Security Baselines to harden the estate. Group Policy will be used to apply the settings. A vulnerability scanner will be used to check the results. These are industry-standard tools widely used for hardening Windows environments.

Why it sits at Pass. Three baselines are named and none is chosen. No comparison is made, so no criterion for choosing is ever stated. "Industry-standard" and "widely used" are generic justifications that would apply to any project in any environment, which is precisely what the Pass descriptor describes. Nothing here is wrong; it is simply not a justification.

Why this is assessed

The lower band of the criterion on method describes work where tools appear with no comparison and no reason that belongs to the field. The top band asks for the opposite: tools that are compared and then chosen, with reasons from the field.

Notice what the notes say about the weak version: nothing in it is wrong. A paragraph can be true in every sentence and still do none of the work that the marks are given for.

Justifying (strong)

Three configuration baselines were considered. DISA STIGs are the most prescriptive but are written for United States federal defence systems, and their assumptions about managed workstation estates do not hold for a small retail SME with mixed hardware and no dedicated administrator. Microsoft Security Baselines align closely with the vendor's own tooling but do not map cleanly onto an external control framework, which this project needs because the evaluation reports against NIST CSF Protect. The CIS Windows Benchmark was selected because it is scoped to organisation size, is expressed as discrete settings that can be applied selectively through Group Policy, and maps to CIS Control 4, which in turn maps to NIST CSF Protect, giving a single traceable line from setting to control to framework function. STIGs are retained only as a comparison point for settings where the two baselines diverge.

Named move: from naming what exists to choosing between alternatives on grounds specific to this environment. The three tells of a real justification are that alternatives are named, a criterion for choosing is stated, and the criterion is derived from the project's own context rather than from general reputation.

Go deeper

Look at the last reason in the strong version: "a single traceable line from setting to control to framework function". The paragraph says that the CIS Windows Benchmark maps to CIS Control 4, and that CIS Control 4 maps to the Protect function of the NIST CSF. To map one thing to another is to show which part of the first matches which part of the second.

This matters because of a sentence earlier in the same paragraph: the evaluation reports against NIST CSF Protect. So the choice of tool is tied to the evaluation. When Chathura reports a result, every setting behind it can be traced to the function that he reports against. A justification of this kind connects two chapters of the proposal. A list cannot.

Read the two passages one after the other. The second is not longer because it is padded; it is longer because it contains an argument, and an argument takes more words than a list.

Phrases explained
not longer because it is padded
The picture: A thin cushion with extra filling pushed in. It is bigger, and no better to sit on.
Why it works: Padding is words that add length and no content. The strong version is longer than the weak one, and the notes want you to see why: each extra sentence does a job. One names an alternative, one gives a test, one ties the test to the project.
Where it stops working: The picture can suggest that short is always better. It is not. The notes do not ask for fewer words. They ask that every sentence does work.
Try it

Find the three tells in Chathura's strong version, and then write your own justification in the same form: open Three Tells.

Check yourself

A proposal says: "Wireshark will be used because it is the industry standard." Which of the three tells are present?

None. No alternative is named, so there is nothing to choose between. "Industry standard" looks like a criterion, but it is a reputation, and it would be equally true in any project. The notes call reasons of this kind generic.

In the strong version, Chathura rejects DISA STIGs. Is the reason that STIGs are a poor baseline?

No. The notes call STIGs the most prescriptive of the three. The reason is fit: STIGs assume a kind of organisation that Pahana Retail is not. The criterion comes from the project's context, which is the third tell. In another project the same criterion could choose STIGs.

The word "baseline" appears in Section 3 and in Section 4. Does it mean the same thing?

No. In Section 3 a configuration baseline is a published set of secure settings, such as the CIS Windows Benchmark. In Section 4 a baseline is what you compare your result with, such as the default configuration. In Chathura's project the first kind is the treatment and the second kind is the comparison.

4. Designing the evaluation

In plain English

An evaluation design has three parts: a metric (what you count), a baseline (what you compare with) and an environment (where the test runs).

The notes then test the main idea of the session. For each of the three students, the three parts are read out of the research question from Session 1.

The rest of the section shows how to choose a metric, gives one complete evaluation design, lists four ways a result can mislead, and ends with a weak passage for you to grade.

Words you will meet in this section

WordWhat it means
UninterpretableCannot be given a meaning. A number with nothing beside it tells you nothing.
UnreproducibleCannot be repeated by another person to check the result.
Comparison clauseThe part of a question that says what the result is compared with. It often begins "compared with".
LatentPresent, but not yet stated openly.
OperationalStated as steps that you can carry out.
Detection rateOf all the real attacks, the share that the system catches.

Every evaluation design needs three things. A metric, which is what you count. A baseline, which is what you compare against. An environment, which is where it runs and why a result obtained there means anything at all.

Each has a matching failure. Without a metric the claim is unmeasurable. Without a baseline the result is uncompared and therefore uninterpretable. Without an environment the work is unreproducible. All three are visible to a marker in a single reading.

BaselineWhat will you set your number beside?Your number stands alone, so nobody can say whether it is good.

4.2 Choosing a domain-appropriate metric

  • Detection work: detection rate and false-positive rate, or precision and recall. Report the pair, never one of them alone.
  • Hardening work: success rate of a defined set of attack techniques, and coverage of that set.
  • Performance and response work: throughput, latency, time-to-containment.
  • Testing methodology work: proportion of known flaws found, and which classes were missed.

Accuracy alone is almost never the right answer in a security context. Ask the degenerate-case question of any metric you propose: what would a system that does nothing score? On an imbalanced detection problem a detector that flags nothing scores highly on accuracy. If doing nothing scores well, the metric is wrong.

Phrases explained
the degenerate-case question
The picture: A smoke alarm with no battery. It never gives a false alarm.
Why it works: A degenerate case is the emptiest possible system: one that does nothing. The question is what your metric gives to that system. If an alarm is scored only on false alarms, the alarm with no battery gets a perfect score. A metric that rewards doing nothing cannot tell you whether your own system is good.
Where it stops working: Nobody would really install an alarm with no battery, and nobody proposes a detector that flags nothing. The empty system is a test of the metric, not a design that anyone wants. Passing this test also does not prove that a metric is right. It only removes one way of being wrong.

4.3 A worked evaluation design

On Dilini at Suwa Sevana Teaching Hospital, a security-testing methodology project. [Illustrative example - do not cite]

The evaluation compares a structured object-level-authorisation testing methodology against unstructured manual testing on a controlled FHIR-style patient-records API. Ground truth is established by seeding a fixed set of authorisation flaws into the API before testing begins: a mixture of horizontal cases, where one patient's record is reachable by another patient's token, and vertical cases, where a clinician role reaches a record outside its assigned care relationship. The seeded set is fixed in advance and is not visible to the tester during the run. The primary metric is the proportion of seeded flaws detected. The controlled condition is a comparable time budget for both approaches, so that the comparison measures the methodology rather than the effort spent. A secondary observation records which flaw classes each approach missed, because a methodology that detects a different distribution of flaws is a more interesting result than one that simply detects more.

A picture to think with: seeded flaws
The picture
A gardener plants four seeds in a bed of nine mounds, and writes down where each one is. The card is the list. The flags show the four places.
Why it works
To seed is to plant. A seeded flaw is a weakness that the researcher places in the test system before the test, and writes down. Because the list exists, the right answer is known in advance: there are exactly this many flaws, in exactly these places. So a result can be counted: this way of testing found three of the four. The notes call the known answer ground truth. Without it you could count what was found, but not what was missed.
Where it stops working
A seed grows and changes. A seeded flaw must not: the notes say that the set is fixed in advance and hidden from the tester during the run. And a gardener's seeds are no different from wild ones, but planted flaws may be easier to find than real ones. The notes name that as a threat to validity. One more limit is about the word. In this module "seeding" also names Weeks 1 to 3, when you find your idea (Session 1 notes, Section 3). That is a different idea. Here, nothing is being started. Something is being hidden so that it can be found.

Three threats to validity are acknowledged. Seeded flaws may be easier to find than naturally occurring ones, which would inflate both arms and narrow the difference between them. A single tester introduces a learning effect across runs, mitigated by running the unstructured arm first. And a controlled API is simpler than a production portal, so the result supports a claim about relative detection rates under controlled conditions rather than a claim about absolute readiness for deployment.

Phrases explained
inflate both arms
The picture: Two balloons, with the same extra air blown into each.
Why it works: The test has two arms: the structured way of testing and the unstructured way. If planted flaws are easy to find, both arms find more of them than they would find in a real system. Both scores rise, and the difference between them gets smaller.
Where it stops working: Two balloons can take the same extra air. Two scores cannot both pass 100 per cent, so the better arm has less room to rise. That is why the difference narrows.
a learning effect across runs
The picture: A person who walks the same path twice knows where the holes are the second time.
Why it works: One tester runs both arms. In the second run the tester already knows the system, so the second arm gets help that it did not earn. Dilini runs the unstructured arm first. Any learning then helps her own structured method, and she says so openly as a limit.
Where it stops working: A walker can forget a path. A tester cannot forget a system on purpose. The order of the runs makes the problem smaller. It does not remove it.

Why this is a Distinction-band evaluation design. It names a metric, a baseline and a controlled condition; it establishes ground truth deliberately rather than assuming it; it reports a secondary observation that would make a null result informative; and it states its own limits in terms of what claim the result will and will not support.

Go deeper

"A secondary observation that would make a null result informative" is worth reading twice. A null result means no difference: suppose both ways of testing find the same share of the planted flaws. With only the main metric, that project has little to report.

Dilini also records which classes of flaw each approach missed. If the two approaches miss different classes, that is a finding even when the totals are equal. When you design your own evaluation, ask: if my main number shows no difference, what will I still have learned?

4.4 Threats to validity, and how to state them

Naming a limitation is a mark of rigour, not a confession. Four threats matter at this level, and each comes with its correction.

Phrases explained
not a confession
The picture: A person admits to something they did wrong, and expects to be punished for it.
Why it works: Many students hide the limits of their work because they think a stated limit will cost marks. The notes say the opposite. Stating a limit shows that you understand your own test.
Where it stops working: A confession is about a fault. A threat to validity is often nobody's fault: every test in a small testbed has limits. What the notes ask for is the limit together with its correction, or with a narrower claim.
  • The testbed does not resemble the target environment. State the divergence and scope the claim to the testbed rather than pretending it generalises.
  • The baseline was chosen because it is easy to beat. Choose the baseline the target environment would actually use, and say why it is the fair comparison.
  • The metric rewards the wrong behaviour. Check the degenerate case: what score does a system that does nothing achieve? If it scores well, the metric is wrong.
  • The dataset or seeded-flaw set leaks the answer. Fix the set in advance, keep it out of sight during the run, and disclose how it was constructed.
Phrases explained
leaks the answer
The picture: A student sees the answer sheet before the examination.
Why it works: If the tester has seen the list of planted flaws, or the detector was trained on the same data that it is tested on, the score measures memory and not ability. The answer has passed from the place where it should be kept to the place where it is being tested.
Where it stops working: In an examination a leak is usually dishonest. Here it is usually an accident, such as one file used for both training and testing. The correction is the same in both cases: fix the set first, keep it out of sight, and say how you made it.

4.5 Discussion excerpt: grade this methodology

This is the passage the room judged in Session 3. Read it before the session if you can, and again afterwards. [Illustrative example - do not cite]

Methodology. This project will develop a machine-learning system to detect malicious network traffic. First, a dataset will be collected. Second, the data will be pre-processed and features will be extracted. Third, several algorithms will be trained, including Random Forest, SVM and a neural network. Python, Scikit-learn and Wireshark will be used, as these are the most popular tools for this kind of work. The system will be tested to confirm that it works, and results will be presented in graphs and tables. The expected outcome is a system with high accuracy that can detect attacks effectively.

Band verdict: Pass, and close to the boundary with Fail on the evaluation dimension.

Work through it in four rounds rather than reacting to it as a whole. Which method family does it name? Which tool is justified, and on what grounds? What is the metric, the baseline and the environment? And then, only then, what is the single edit that moves it up a band?

Try it

Grade the passage round by round before you read the answers below: open Four Rounds.

Method. The passage never names a method family. It describes a sequence of build steps, which is the exact failure the session opens on. It is a plan for constructing something, not a design for finding something out.

Tools. Three tools are named and none is justified. "Most popular" is a reputation argument, not a domain-specific one, and it would sit unchanged in any project in any environment. Three algorithms are listed with no basis for comparison and no statement of what would make one preferable.

Evaluation. This is the weakest dimension and the one to spend time on. "Tested to confirm that it works" is not an evaluation; it presumes the conclusion. There is no baseline, so "high accuracy" is uncompared and therefore uninterpretable. There is no environment, so the result is unreproducible. And "accuracy" on a detection problem with imbalanced classes is the wrong metric, since a detector that flags nothing scores well on it. Do not let the room stop at "add a baseline": press for which baseline and why.

The one edit. Name a metric, a baseline and an environment, in that order, and let the evaluation design follow from them. Do not accept "use a bigger dataset", "try more algorithms" or "add more graphs": none of these is a Criterion 3 lever, and each is the kind of answer that feels productive while leaving the band unchanged.

Phrases explained
a Criterion 3 lever
The picture: A long bar moves a heavy stone, if you push at the right place.
Why it works: A lever is a small action with a large effect. Naming a metric, a baseline and an environment is a small edit that changes how the whole section reads. A bigger dataset, more algorithms or more graphs are large efforts that change nothing a marker looks for under this criterion.
Where it stops working: A real lever multiplies your force with certainty. An edit does not. Naming the three parts gives your section a chance at a higher band, and the parts still have to be the right ones.

Notice which of the four rounds took longest. Almost everyone finds the tool problem within seconds and the evaluation problem only after being pushed. That asymmetry is the reason this session exists.

Why this is assessed

Evaluation is one of the things that the criterion on method looks at. At the top, the evaluation has criteria that can be measured and that suit the field. Lower down, it is present and loose.

The notes explain why this session exists: nearly everyone sees a weak tool choice at once, and a weak evaluation only when pushed. So when you check your own draft, begin with the part that you are least likely to see.

Check yourself

A proposal reports: "The detector reached 97 per cent accuracy." Name two things that the notes would ask about this result.

Why does Dilini plant flaws in the API, and not simply test it and count what she finds?

5. Designing the system

In plain English

Your proposal needs a sketch of the system that you will build. The notes give four things the sketch must show, and one test: could you hand it to someone else to build?

The fourth thing is the one most often forgotten: the place in the system where you take your measurement.

The section ends with the testbed: the small system that you build yourself, so that the test is safe and can be repeated.

An architecture sketch is credibly implementation-ready at Level 6 when its components are named by function rather than by product, its data flow is shown, its trust boundaries are marked, and its point of measurement is identified. The working test is whether you could hand it to someone else to build.

A marked trust boundary is a design claim, not decoration. Zero trust architecture (Rose et al., 2020) starts from the position that no component is trusted because of where it sits on the network: access to each resource is authenticated and authorised per session, rather than granted because the request comes from inside. Read against your own sketch, that position asks one question. Wherever you have drawn a trusted internal zone, what in your design enforces the trust, and does your evaluation test it? Dilini's project at Suwa Sevana Teaching Hospital is the clearest case: a patient-portal API that serves any record to any authenticated caller has extended trust it never checks, and that implicit trust is exactly what her object-level authorisation testing is designed to expose.

Phrases explained
a design claim, not decoration
The picture: A line on a floor plan that says "wall". The builder must build a wall there.
Why it works: When you draw a boundary and mark one side as trusted, you are claiming that something in your design makes that true. A reader may ask what enforces it. If nothing does, the line is only decoration.
Where it stops working: A wall on a plan is either built or not built. Trust is less simple. It can be checked for each request, each time, which is the position of zero trust that the notes describe.
Go deeper

"Rose et al., 2020" is a citation of a real document, NIST SP 800-207. Its full reference is at the end of these notes. "Et al." is short for the Latin for "and others": in the text, a source with three or more authors is cited by the first author's name followed by these words.

Look at how the notes use the source. They do not only report what zero trust is. They turn it into a question for your own sketch: wherever you have drawn a trusted zone, what enforces the trust, and does your evaluation test it? That is the engaging move from Session 2 notes, Section 3, applied to a design.

5.1 The worked sketch

Tharindu's detection project at Sevana Bank runs as a four-stage pipeline: a traffic source, a feature extraction stage, a scoring model, and a decision output. The measurement point is not a fifth stage. It is attached at the boundary between scoring and decision, because that is where a score becomes a decision and therefore where a false positive is created and can be counted. Where you attach the measurement is an argument you have to make, not a detail you can leave implicit.

Phrases explained
a four-stage pipeline
The picture: Water runs through a pipe in one direction, and passes four stations on the way.
Why it works: In a pipeline, each stage takes what the stage before it produced, does one job, and passes the result on. Data moves one way. That makes the design easy to draw and easy to reason about: you can ask, at each join, what exactly passes across.
Where it stops working: Water in a pipe stays water. Data in a pipeline changes at every stage: traffic becomes features, features become a score, and a score becomes a decision. That is why the place of measurement matters. A false positive does not exist until the last change.

5.2 Common architecture failures

  • A diagram with no data flow. Boxes with no arrows describe an inventory, not a design.
  • A design that cannot be built in the time available. Feasibility is assessed under Criterion 3 and again under Criterion 4.
  • A component named after a product rather than a function. Products change; functions are what you are designing.
  • A sketch with no point of measurement. This is the one that silently kills the evaluation, because it is invisible until the marking.
Phrases explained
silently kills the evaluation
The picture: A slow leak in a tyre. Nothing is heard, and the car stops later, far from home.
Why it works: The other three failures can be seen on the drawing by anyone who looks. This one cannot, because a sketch with no point of measurement still looks complete. The damage appears later, when you try to collect a number and find that the design gives you no place to collect it.
Where it stops working: A leak happens to you. This failure does not: it is an omission, and you can prevent it today by marking the point on your sketch and saying why it is there.

5.3 The testbed

A testbed is not a scale model of the target network and is not trying to be. It is a room from which you have removed everything you are not measuring, so that when the number changes you know why. Reproducibility is the point: another person, given your description, should be able to rebuild it and obtain a comparable result.

A picture to think with: a controlled room
The picture
A room with almost nothing in it: one device on a table, and one dial on the wall.
Why it works
In a full room, many things could move the needle on the dial. In this room only one thing can. So when the needle moves, you know the cause. A testbed works in the same way: you leave out everything that you are not measuring, so that a change in your number has one explanation. A room this simple can also be described in a few lines, which is what lets another person rebuild it and check your result.
Where it stops working
An empty room is easy to imagine and hard to build. Deciding what to leave out is itself a design decision, and you have to defend it. And a result from a simple room is a result about that room. The notes say so in Section 4.4: where the testbed differs from the real environment, state the difference and scope the claim to the testbed.
Phrases explained
not a scale model
The picture: A small copy of a building, with every room and window in the right place.
Why it works: A scale model tries to copy everything, smaller. Many students think that a testbed should copy the real network in this way, and then feel that their small testbed is a poor copy. The notes remove that worry: copying is not the aim.
Where it stops working: A scale model is judged by how closely it matches the original. A testbed is judged by something else: whether it lets you measure one thing cleanly, and whether another person can rebuild it.

Everything in this module runs in a self-built virtualised testbed or laboratory environment. Nothing is directed at a live third-party system. Treat that as a design constraint from the start rather than something to negotiate later.

ThroughputHow much work a system handles in a given time.
LatencyThe delay between a request and its response.
Time-to-containmentHow long it takes to stop an attack from spreading.
Ground truthThe right answer, known in advance, that you check a result against.
Seeded flawA weakness placed in a test system on purpose, so that you can count how many a method finds. It is not the same as seeding (Section 1), which is about finding your idea.
Object-level authorisationThe check that a user may open this one record, not only that the user has signed in.
TokenA piece of data that proves to a system who the caller is.
Controlled conditionSomething that you keep the same for both sides of a comparison, so that it cannot explain the difference.
Time budgetA fixed amount of time allowed for a task.
ArmOne side of a comparison. A test of two approaches has two arms.
Null resultA result that shows no difference between the two arms.
Threat to validityA reason why a result could mislead you or your reader.
InflateTo make a number larger than it should be.
MitigateTo make a problem smaller.
GeneraliseTo claim that a result found in one place holds in other places too.
Scope a claimTo say exactly where a claim holds, and to claim nothing beyond that.
Architecture sketchA drawing of the parts of a system and how they connect.
Implementation-readyDetailed enough that someone could start building from it.
ComponentOne part of a system.
FunctionWhat a part does. "Scoring model" is a function. The name of a product you could buy is not.
Data flowThe path that data takes from one part to the next, shown by arrows.
Trust boundaryA line in a design. On one side, something is trusted. On the other side, it is not.
Point of measurementThe place in the system where you take the number for your metric.
Zero trustA way of designing systems in which nothing is trusted only because of where it is on the network.
AuthenticatedChecked for identity: the system knows who is calling.
AuthorisedChecked for permission: the system knows that this caller may do this thing.
Per sessionAgain for each new connection, not once for all time.
Implicit trustTrust that is given without any check.
Feature extractionTurning raw data into the measured properties that a model uses.
InventoryA list of things that you have.
TestbedA small system that you build yourself in order to run tests safely.
VirtualisedRunning as virtual machines, which are computers made in software inside a real computer.
Third-partyBelonging to someone other than you and your university.
Live systemA system that real people are using now.
RubricThe table that markers use. It has one row for each criterion and one column for each band.
DescriptorThe text in one cell of the rubric. It describes work in one band of one criterion.
BandA range of marks with a name, such as Distinction or Pass.
ParaphraseThe same meaning in different words.
UndefendedStated with no argument to support it.
CharacteristicTypical of a person or a group.
ExtractTo take out. Here: to read the three parts out of your question and write them down.
ExplicitlyStated openly, so that nobody has to guess.
AppendixA section at the end of a document that holds supporting material.
ReferenceThe full details of a source, so that a reader can find it.
Et al.Short for the Latin for "and others". It is used in the text when a source has three or more authors.
